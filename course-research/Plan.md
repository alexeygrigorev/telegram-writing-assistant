# Plan: Audience-First Discovery → First Course MVP

## Current strategy

I am not starting from zero. I already have an audience and distribution, so I do not want to begin by inventing a narrow ideal customer profile and then searching for that audience from scratch.

Instead, I want to start from the audience I already have, learn who among them has the strongest relevant problems, and refine the offer based on what I hear and observe.

The working sequence is:

> **Existing audience → conversations → problem patterns → initial offer → first cohort MVP → outcome analysis → narrower audience / sharper offer**

This is intentionally broader at the beginning, but it should not be unstructured.

## Core hypothesis

The initial hypothesis is that some people in my existing audience have recurring business processes that still depend too much on them or their team, and that AI can help turn those processes into more reliable, repeatable workflows.

I do not yet want to assume that the best audience is education/community businesses, agencies, consultants, or another vertical. I want to discover which subset of my existing audience has the strongest pain, willingness to act, and ability to get value from the approach.

The first product hypothesis remains approximately:

> **Turn one recurring business process into a tested AI-assisted workflow that you and/or your team can run reliably.**

The audience can remain relatively broad at first, but the transformation should stay concrete.

# Phase 1: Talk to the audience before finalizing the offer

## Goal

Understand the actual problems, language, workflows, and current alternatives of people already in my orbit before locking down the positioning and curriculum.

The objective is not to ask people what AI course they want. The objective is to understand their work.

## Call to action

Invite people from the existing audience to talk.

The invitation should be broad enough to attract unexpected but relevant people. A possible framing:

> I am researching how people are using AI in real business operations beyond one-off prompting and content generation. I am especially interested in recurring work that still takes too much founder/operator attention. If this sounds familiar, I would love to talk for 20–30 minutes and understand how you work today.

Do not sell the course aggressively at this stage. The conversation itself is research.

## Interview questions

Focus on past and current behavior rather than hypothetical feature requests.

### Context

- What do you do?
- What kind of business/team are you part of?
- What is your role?
- How large is the team?
- How are you currently using AI?

### Recurring work

- What recurring work do you personally spend more time on than you would like?
- What recurring process still depends on you?
- Tell me about the last time you did it.
- What triggers the process?
- Who is involved?
- Which tools are involved?
- What information do you need?
- Which decisions still require you?

### Pain

- Where does the process become annoying, slow, or unreliable?
- What tends to get forgotten or go wrong?
- How often does this happen?
- Roughly how much time does it consume?
- What happens if you are unavailable?
- What is the business consequence when it fails or is delayed?

### Existing alternatives

- What have you already tried?
- Have you tried delegating it?
- Have you tried automation / Zapier / n8n / scripts / AI tools?
- What worked?
- What did not work?
- Why is the problem still unsolved?

### Desired outcome

- If this process worked extremely well without requiring your constant attention, what would change?
- How valuable would that be?
- What would you never want AI to decide or do automatically?

Avoid asking primarily:

> “What would you like to learn about AI?”

That produces solution-space answers such as agents, prompts, MCP, Codex, n8n, etc. The research should first uncover problems and jobs-to-be-done.

# Phase 3: Refine the first offer from the conversations

After enough conversations, synthesize the most common high-value problem patterns.

Possible patterns might be:

- founder is still the routing / approval layer;
- recurring reporting takes too much time;
- customer or learner follow-up is inconsistent;
- program / event operations require too much manual coordination;
- team has SOPs but execution still depends on memory/context;
- AI is already used for one-off work but not as a reliable operating workflow;
- automations exist but are brittle and hard to trust.

Then refine the offer around the strongest problem pattern rather than around AI technology.

A likely broad first-cohort positioning:

> **Bring one recurring business process that still requires too much of your attention. Over the course, turn it into a specified, tested AI-assisted workflow that you or your team can run reliably.**

This keeps the industry/role relatively broad while making the job-to-be-done specific.

Avoid a positioning that is broad on both dimensions, such as:

> “Build your AI-native business.”

That can remain the larger category or long-term vision, but the first product should have a much more concrete transformation.

# Phase 4: First edition of the course = MVP

The first edition of the course is the MVP, but the thing being validated is not primarily the curriculum.

The MVP should test the **transformation**:

> Can I repeatedly help people turn an important recurring process from founder-/human-dependent into a reliable human + AI workflow?

The curriculum is the current delivery mechanism for that transformation.

The first cohort should therefore be unusually hands-on and flexible. It is acceptable to do things manually that would not scale later.

The first edition should optimize for learning rather than polish.

## Possible delivery method

Use the existing framework:

> **Map → Specify → Build → Prove → Run**

### Map

Choose a recurring workflow worth improving and establish the current baseline.

Questions:

- Is it frequent?
- Is it expensive in time or attention?
- Does it have clear inputs/outputs?
- Does it currently depend on the founder/operator?
- Is there enough economic value to justify solving it?

### Specify

Turn implicit knowledge into an explicit operating specification:

- trigger;
- inputs;
- source material/context;
- decisions;
- rules;
- exceptions;
- quality criteria;
- approval points;
- definition of done;
- output destination.

### Build

Create the smallest working solution. Do not require a particular technology.

The implementation may combine:

- AI;
- manual steps;
- SaaS tools;
- scripts/code;
- automations;
- human review.

The goal is not “build an agent.” The goal is to solve the workflow.

### Prove

Test the workflow against real or representative cases.

Measure:

- correctness;
- failures;
- exceptions;
- human intervention required;
- quality;
- reliability.

### Run

Put it into real operation.

Define:

- workflow owner;
- monitoring;
- approvals;
- exception handling;
- maintenance;
- what the AI may decide;
- what remains a human decision.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | | | | | |

Important signals:

### Who applies?

This shows which parts of the existing audience recognize themselves in the problem.

### Who pays?

This is stronger evidence that the problem matters.

### Who actually succeeds?

This is potentially the strongest segmentation signal.

The ideal customer may not be the largest group in the audience. It may be the group that achieves the strongest measurable transformation with the method.

### Who wants to do it again?

A particularly strong product signal is:

> After completing workflow #1, does the participant immediately want to apply the same method to workflow #2?

If yes, the value may not just be one workflow. The deeper product may be a repeatable operating method.

# Success metrics for cohort 1

Do not rely mainly on course metrics such as:

- videos watched;
- attendance;
- assignments completed;
- number of agents built;
- NPS alone.

Measure operational change.

Possible before/after measures:

- founder interventions per run;
- human hours per run;
- cycle time;
- mistakes / rework;
- missed follow-ups;
- turnaround time;
- number of people required;
- capacity / throughput;
- reliability across repeated runs.

A strong validation event is:

> **The workflow runs successfully in a real situation without requiring the founder to execute the process manually.**

# How the audience can narrow over time

Do not force a narrow vertical before the evidence exists.

Instead:

### Cohort 1

**Question:** Who is this really for, and which problems produce the most value?

Broad audience, narrow transformation.

### Cohort 2

**Question:** Does the strongest customer/problem cluster repeat?

Sharpen the audience and examples around the best cluster.

### Cohort 3

**Question:** Can the transformation be standardized and delivered repeatedly?

Turn the successful patterns into templates, diagnostics, examples, and a more fixed curriculum.

### Later

Scale acquisition, strengthen positioning, and test premium/company pricing once the transformation and customer profile are clearer.

---

# Current strategic principle

The approach is not simply:

> audience → course

It is:

> **Existing audience → conversations → understand real work and pain → identify opportunity clusters → make a concrete offer → run the first course as an MVP → observe who buys and who succeeds → refine the audience and product.**

The important constraint is:

> **Be broad about who is allowed to come, but specific about the problem/transformation being explored.**

This preserves the advantage of existing distribution without turning the first cohort into an unstructured mix of unrelated AI use cases.
