# AI-Native Business OS / AI-Assisted Automation Premium Program Research

**Date:** 2026-08-29  
**Status:** Working research memo

## Executive summary

The strongest opportunity is not to sell a generic AI course, a Codex course, or a multi-agent course. The most defensible premium offer is to help nontechnical founder-operators and senior operators turn one recurring, founder-dependent business process into a tested workflow that humans and AI can run together.

The central positioning hypothesis is:

> **Build one AI-run workflow in four weeks. Turn a recurring process that depends on you into a tested workflow your team and AI can operate together—without becoming a programmer.**

The credibility story is unusually strong because the instructor is already operating two communities, many courses across many platforms, with two people plus a team of agents. DataOps is the reference implementation that demonstrates the underlying operating principles, but it should not be sold as the required software stack. Participants should learn a less technical framework they can adapt to their own tools and business.

A useful high-level method is:

> **Map → Specify → Build → Prove → Run**

The product should be priced and delivered as a company-level implementation program, not as content-only training. At a $10,000 price point, the company is paying for workflow selection, implementation guidance, safety/reliability review, direct feedback, deployment of one real workflow, and internal capability transfer.

# 1. Strategic direction

## 1.1 What the offer should not be

Avoid positioning the program as:

- “Learn ChatGPT.”
- “Learn Codex.”
- “Learn Claude Code.”
- “Build four AI agents.”
- “AI automation for business people.”
- “No-code AI for everyone.”

Those categories are crowded, price-compared to much cheaper courses, and tied too closely to tools that will change quickly.

## 1.2 What the offer should become

The more durable category is:

> **AI-native operations for founder-led businesses.**

The durable capability participants should leave with is:

> **The ability to repeatedly transform a messy recurring process into a specified, tested, human-supervised AI workflow.**

The strongest current headline is:

> ## **Build one AI-run workflow in four weeks**
> Turn a recurring process that depends on you into a tested workflow your team and AI can operate together—without becoming a programmer.

A broader category-level message can sit above it:

> **Build your AI-native business operating system.**

# 2. Market opportunity

## 2.1 AI adoption is broad, but operational integration is still rare

A March 2026 Goldman Sachs survey of 1,256 small-business owners reported that 76% were using AI and 93% of AI users reported a positive business impact. 84% cited efficiency or productivity gains. However, only 14% said AI was fully integrated into core operations, while 73% wanted more training and implementation resources. Leading barriers included technical expertise, choosing the right tools, and privacy concerns.

Source: https://www.goldmansachs.com/pressroom/press-releases/2026/small-businesses-embrace-ai-but-need-training-and-support-to-fully-harness-it

An ECB survey of 5,067 euro-area firms—92% of them employing fewer than 250 people—showed a similar integration gap. Only 7% used AI significantly. 31% used it moderately, 33% very infrequently, and 27% not at all. SMEs were more likely than larger firms to be non-users.

Source: https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2026/html/ecb.pr260202~71c8405376.da.html

The market is therefore not primarily “people who need convincing that AI matters.” The stronger audience is:

> People who already believe AI is useful, may already pay for ChatGPT or Claude, but have not converted AI access into reliable operating capacity.

## 2.2 Knowledge workers are moving into Codex

OpenAI reported in June 2026 that Codex had more than five million weekly users. Knowledge workers represented around 20% of users and were growing more than three times as fast as developers. Publicly described use cases include reports, spreadsheets, presentations, contracts, research, data analysis, workflow automation, lightweight tools, and moving work through review and approval.

Source: https://openai.com/index/codex-for-knowledge-work/

This supports a nontechnical audience. They do not need to become programmers, but they do need to become competent at:

- describing work precisely;
- organizing context;
- providing examples;
- defining what “good” and “done” mean;
- reviewing changes;
- managing permissions and risk;
- measuring whether the system actually helps.

This is closer to product management and operations management than traditional software development.

This is a purposive sample rather than a representative survey. LinkedIn overrepresents polished success claims; Reddit overrepresents breakages and skepticism; X, TikTok, Instagram, private Slack groups, and private communities are imperfectly indexed. The percentages below should therefore be interpreted as directional patterns in visible discourse.

## 3.1 How many people appear to have genuinely solved something?

| Type of public account | Count | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Detailed working implementation | 8 | 14% |
| Working but thin/self-reported claim | 11 | 20% |

| Pain described without working solution | 9 | 16% |
| Failure, warning, or postmortem | 8 | 14% |
| Framework/discussion/demand signal | 8 | 14% |

Combined, 19 of 56 posts (34%) described something that appeared to be working. Only 8 of 56 (14%) contained enough detail about workflow, inputs, constraints, process, or results to learn much from the implementation.

This is an important signal: there is abundant excitement and claimed success, but relatively little public evidence of repeatable, safe systems.

## 3.2 Most common problems in the public sample

Themes overlapped.

| Theme | Posts mentioning it | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Repetitive operational work | 22 | 39% |
| Reliability, security, or maintenance | 12 | 21% |
| Process clarity, requirements, or specifications | 12 | 21% |
| Founder or small-team capacity | 10 | 18% |
| Technical dependency or overwhelm | 8 | 14% |
| Tool sprawl and integration friction | 6 | 11% |

Reporting was the most repeated concrete workflow, appearing in 12 posts. Follow-up appeared in six. Coding agents appeared in eight and n8n in five.

Reporting is a particularly attractive first-project category because it is frequent, structured, expensive in aggregate, and relatively easy for a human to review.

## 3.3 What successful users were doing differently

The strongest public stories repeatedly used some combination of:

1. A clearly bounded task.
2. Existing examples or source material.
3. An explicit definition of done.
4. A persistent project workspace.
5. A backlog or workflow tracker.
6. Human review at consequential points.
7. Testing against representative cases.
8. Small iterations rather than one giant autonomous run.
9. Clear separation between what AI decides and what a human decides.
10. Version history and rollback capability.

The weak stories tended to stop at “I built a demo.” The stronger stories explained how work was specified, reviewed, tested, handed off, or maintained.

# 4. Audience definition

## 4.1 Recommended beachhead

The strongest initial audience is:

> **Nontechnical founder-operators and operations leaders in founder-led, knowledge-intensive businesses with recurring delivery across multiple tools.**

High-fit businesses include:

- education companies running courses, cohorts, workshops, certifications, and communities;
- community and media businesses running memberships, newsletters, events, podcasts, sponsorships, and content;
- agencies and consultancies with recurring client onboarding, delivery, reporting, reviews, and follow-ups;
- coaching, training, and professional-services businesses with repeated intake, preparation, delivery, documentation, and customer-success processes.

The company-size qualifier is less important than operational complexity. A two-person community business can have more workflow complexity than a 30-person software company.

## 4.2 Organizational characteristics of the ideal customer

| Characteristic | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Multiple recurring offers | Courses, memberships, client projects, events, content, coaching, sponsorships |
| Operational tool sprawl | Email, spreadsheets, calendar, CRM, community platform, course platform, docs, chat, payments |
| Founder as coordination layer | Approvals, exceptions, reminders, and “what happens next?” still come through the founder |
| Repeated knowledge work | Reports, research, drafts, preparation documents, summaries, onboarding, follow-up |
| Some process maturity | Existing SOPs, templates, examples, checklists, or experienced staff—even if inconsistent |
| Economic pain | At least one workflow consumes several expensive hours every week or creates recurring delays/mistakes |
| Human judgment still matters | Relationships, approvals, strategic decisions, or quality control cannot be eliminated |
| Willingness to build | They want internal capability, not only a consultant’s black box |

## 4.3 Buyer and builder may be different people

For a $10,000 company-level program, the ideal enrollment may include two people:

- Founder/CEO/business-unit leader who owns the outcome and budget.
- Operator/COO/chief of staff/operations manager/community lead/senior assistant who will maintain the workflow.

This is stronger than training the founder alone. Otherwise, the founder can accidentally become the full-time supervisor of every AI workflow.

## 4.4 Top three initial segments

### Segment 1: Education, community, and media businesses

This is the strongest credibility match because the instructor already understands the operational reality of courses, cohorts, learners, speakers, guests, events, sponsors, content, publishing, platforms, support, reminders, and recurring calendars.

Circle’s 2026 community research found that 48% of creators ran everything themselves, 19% led small community-focused teams, and 27% used six or more tools. AI usage was concentrated in content and planning: 75% used it there, compared with 34% for member support and 18% for moderation agents. 45% reported signs of member burnout and 27% felt pressure to prove ROI.

Source: https://circle.so/de/blog/creator-economy-statistics

This describes a high-operational-burden, high-AI-awareness market with shallow integration.

### Segment 2: Agencies and consultancies

Common recurring workflows include:

- reporting;
- lead follow-up;
- proposals;
- onboarding;
- content production;
- meeting summaries;
- CRM updates;
- client communication;
- project setup;
- QA;
- invoicing preparation.

Their advantage as a market is economic clarity. ROI can be expressed in staff hours, delivery time, reporting turnaround, missed follow-ups, accounts per operator, and gross margin.

### Segment 3: Founder-led professional or membership businesses

This includes coaching companies, training providers, associations, fractional-service businesses, and expert-led memberships. They combine recurring revenue with high-touch service and face a central tension:

> Scale operations without making the customer/member experience feel automated.

Circle’s 2025 report argued that AI should support human relationships rather than replace them. Only 19% of respondents were already using chat agents, although 70% were considering or planning them.

Source: https://circle.so/fr/2025-community-trends-report

# 5. Problems the audience recognizes

## 5.1 “Everything still comes through me”

The founder acts as the organization’s routing system:

- deciding priorities;
- answering exceptions;
- checking quality;
- remembering follow-ups;
- explaining context;
- finding documents;
- confirming completion;
- connecting tools and people.

AI can initially make this worse. If execution becomes faster while approvals and review remain centralized, the founder can become an even more concentrated bottleneck.

Public example:
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jamesschramko_you-save-money-you-10x-your-output-you-activity-7448233312432726016-rgIm

## 5.2 “We have too many tools, but no operating system”

The typical business has fragments of process across email, Slack, WhatsApp, spreadsheets, CRM, calendars, dashboards, docs, and SaaS platforms. The founder or operator becomes the middleware connecting them.

Public example:
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/darren-bekker-3229103_most-sme-founders-are-the-bottleneck-not-activity-7465694884646223872-g3uj

Circle also reported that only 6% of its 2025 community survey had no plans to consolidate tools.

Source: https://circle.so/fr/2025-community-trends-report

The course should not promise to replace all software. It should teach how to create a coherent workflow across the minimum necessary systems.

## 5.3 “We use AI, but mostly for content”

A common maturity pattern is:

1. Drafting posts, emails, summaries, and ideas.
2. One-off prompts.
3. No durable workflow.
4. No persistent project context.
5. No explicit quality gate.
6. No maintenance system.

The next maturity step is not “better prompting.” It is turning AI into part of a persistent operation:

- defined input;
- context;
- rules;
- execution;
- output;
- exception handling;
- evidence;
- repetition.

## 5.4 “We have SOPs, but work still depends on memory”

Traditional SOPs often omit:

- trigger;
- required inputs;
- decision rules;
- exceptions;
- evidence;
- quality thresholds;
- missing-information behavior;
- approval responsibility;
- output location.

The course should teach participants to convert domain expertise into an **agent-readable operating specification**.

## 5.5 “I can make a demo, but I don’t trust it”

A recurring failure pattern in AI-built systems is the “almost complete” system: the happy path works, but security, authorization, cost limits, input limits, error handling, and maintainability are missing.

Reddit example:
https://nl.reddit.com/user/c0decracker_/comments/1u79cao/the_trap_of_almost_done_vibecoded_apps/

The equivalent in business workflows is:

- malformed spreadsheet breaks the run;
- missing field creates a plausible but wrong result;
- agent sends something without approval;
- source structure changes;
- scheduled run silently fails;
- no rollback exists;
- creator leaves and nobody can maintain it.

Reliability therefore deserves a full curriculum week.

## 5.6 “I do not want to become a programmer”

The promise should not be “coding is now easy.” The better promise is:

> You will learn enough about files, versions, tests, permissions, and system boundaries to direct and supervise Codex safely.

Participants do not need syntax fluency. They do need system literacy.

# 7. Instructor credibility and DataOps reference implementation

The credibility story should be framed around the real operation:

> **Two people operate two communities, many courses, events, content streams, and platforms with the help of specialized agents.**

DataOps should be used as the reference implementation of the principles, not as mandatory software for participants.

## 7.1 What DataOps demonstrates

### Structured process knowledge

SOPs, templates, references, playbooks, prompts, and machine-readable documents. GitHub is the source of truth for process knowledge while execution state is separate.

### Executable workflows

Reusable Template → workflow/Card → Tasks → due dates → required links/files → stages → execution state.

### Operations-first interface

The V1 goal is centered on what the operator must act on: today, overdue, waiting, follow-ups, recurring work, and at-risk workflows—not merely browsing docs.

### “Done” has semantics

Completion can require a URL, file, comment, or other proof. Work should not disappear just because someone clicked a checkbox.

### AI assistants are workflow components

The podcast assistant processes operational input, can use Codex or Claude as the engine, creates artifacts, and participates in the broader operations workflow.

### Agent roles and quality gates

The DataOps development process uses specialized roles: Product Manager, Software Engineer, Tester, On-Call, Architect, Process Curator, Assistant Engineer, etc., with explicit gates and handoffs.

## 7.2 Business OS vs building OS

There are effectively two operating systems.

### Business OS

- workflow templates;
- tasks;
- follow-ups;
- SOPs;
- artifacts;
- assistants;
- recurring work;
- proof;
- attention queues.

### Building OS

- intake;
- PM grooming;
- engineering;
- testing;
- PM acceptance;
- commit;
- deploy;
- on-call.

The course should focus first on the Business OS principles. The Building OS can be shown as an advanced example of how the same operating principles extend to software/agent teams.

## 7.3 DataOps should be a case study, not the required stack

Do not say:

> “Deploy DataOps.”

Instead say:

> “DataOps is the reference implementation of the principles we use. You will build the equivalent operating capability using the simplest tools appropriate for your business.”

One participant may use GitHub + ChatGPT + Codex + Gmail + HubSpot. Another may use Notion + Slack + Airtable + Codex. Another may use Google Drive + spreadsheets + agents.

The transferable IP is how to model and automate the company, not a particular AWS stack.

## 1. Capture

Where does work come from?

- email;
- Telegram;
- forms;

- calendar;
- people;
- scheduled triggers.

## 2. Codify

How should the work happen?

- SOPs;
- rules;
- templates;
- examples;
- context;
- acceptance criteria.

## 3. Orchestrate

What happens next?

- workflow;
- stages;
- tasks;
- owners;
- due dates;
- dependencies;
- waiting;
- follow-up.

## 4. Automate

Which steps should software or AI perform?

- scripts;
- APIs;
- coding agents;
- LLM calls;
- assistants;
- deterministic workflows.

## 5. Verify

How do we know it worked?

- links;
- files;
- tests;

- evidence;
- human gates.

## 6. Operate

How does the business know what needs attention?

- today;
- overdue;
- waiting;
- follow-up;
- at risk;
- failed automation.

## 7. Improve

When something goes wrong, update the system rather than relying on tribal knowledge.

# 11. Four-week curriculum

Four weeks is recommended instead of three because nontechnical participants need one full iteration between a working prototype and a system they trust.

## Week 0 — onboarding and workflow selection

Participants configure:

- ChatGPT desktop;
- Codex desktop app;
- provided starter workspace;
- GitHub Desktop or another visual version-history mechanism;
- appropriate privacy settings.

They bring three candidate workflows and representative examples.

### Deliverables

- current tool map;
- candidate workflow list;
- five representative input/output examples;
- baseline estimate of time, frequency, delays, and mistakes;
- data-sensitivity classification.

---

## Week 1 — Find the leverage workflow

### Theme: Map

Teach participants to see their business as recurring workflows rather than tools or job titles.

Map:

- triggers;
- inputs;
- steps;
- decisions;
- handoffs;
- waiting states;
- outputs;
- evidence;
- exceptions;
- owners;
- frequency;
- delay/error costs.

### Core exercise: “Show me what happened last time”

Participants walk through a real completed case:

- What started it?
- What arrived?
- What was missing?
- What did you copy/paste?
- Whom did you chase?
- What decisions did you make?
- Where did you look for context?
- What did the customer/member receive?
- How did you know it was done?

### Workflow scorecard

Rank workflows by:

- frequency;
- senior time consumed;
- delay cost;
- error cost;
- input consistency;
- clarity of good output;
- reversibility;
- data sensitivity;
- integration complexity.

### DataOps case-study concepts

- waiting vs follow-up due;
- completion requires proof;
- process instructions should appear next to work;
- dashboard should surface attention, not merely store docs.

### Week 1 deliverables

- selected workflow;
- current-state workflow map;
- baseline metric;
- first definition of done;
- automation boundary: human vs deterministic software vs AI.

---

## Week 2 — Make the work agent-readable

### Theme: Specify

Participants create an **Agent Brief**.

| Section | Definition |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Why the workflow exists |
| Trigger | What starts it |
| Inputs | Required/optional information |
| Context | Policies, examples, tone, customer information |
| Procedure | Ordered steps |
| Decisions | Rules and judgment points |
| Exceptions | Missing/conflicting/unusual inputs |
| Outputs | Files, drafts, records, summaries, queues |
| Definition of done | Observable completion criteria |
| Evidence | What must be saved |
| Authority | What the agent may do automatically |
| Escalation | When a human must decide |
| Tests | Examples that must pass |

### Evaluation set

Participants create:

- five ordinary cases;
- two incomplete cases;
- two edge cases;
- one deliberately misleading or unsafe case.

### ChatGPT’s role

Use ChatGPT to:

- interview the process owner;
- normalize the SOP;
- identify ambiguity;
- generate edge cases;
- propose acceptance criteria;
- convert historical examples into tests;
- explain technical decisions in business language.

### Codex’s first role

Codex creates the smallest useful operator, for example:

- structured report generator;
- intake processor;
- follow-up queue;
- research/briefing package;
- content preparation workflow;
- simple internal interface;
- repeatable document or spreadsheet process.

No scheduling yet. No multi-agent system. No unsupervised external writes.

### Week 2 deliverables

- complete Agent Brief;
- ten-case evaluation set;
- working local prototype;
- reviewed sample outputs.

---

## Week 3 — Build the reliable workflow

### Theme: Build and Prove

Connect limited real work in a deliberate progression:

1. files manually placed in inbox folder;
2. CSV/spreadsheet export;
3. controlled read-only connector;
4. only then a write action with approval.

Teach:

- version history;
- logs;
- retries;
- missing-input handling;
- cost limits;
- permissions;
- data minimization;
- rollback;
- approval gates;
- plain-language errors;
- testing after every change.

Public practitioner discussions repeatedly suggest treating agents as fast junior contributors rather than autonomous architects: clearly scoped tasks, explicit constraints, isolated work, and tests outperform broad instructions.

Example discussion:
https://np.reddit.com/r/ClaudeAI/comments/1rozbqb/are_agents_actually_useful_for_complex_tasks/

### Mandatory failure drill

Test:

- missing source file;
- wrong file type;
- contradictory instructions;
- oversized input;
- unavailable service;
- low-confidence result;
- duplicate run;
- dangerous request.

The workflow must fail visibly and safely.

### Week 3 deliverables

- pilot-ready workflow;
- test report;
- human-approval design;
- run log;
- recovery procedure;
- privacy/permissions checklist.

---

## Week 4 — Put it into operation

### Theme: Run

Only now introduce scheduling, repeated operation, or additional agents.

OpenAI’s Codex app supports project threads, parallel agents, reusable skills, and scheduled Automations whose results arrive in a review queue.

Source:
https://openai.com/index/introducing-the-codex-app/

Participants:

- package instructions as a reusable skill;
- assign a human owner;
- schedule where appropriate;
- define what appears in the review queue;
- hand operation to another team member;
- run on live work;
- record cycle time, human time, errors, and exceptions;
- conduct a retrospective;
- create a 90-day backlog.

### Week 4 deliverables

- one live workflow;
- operator runbook;
- owner/escalation path;
- before-and-after measurement;
- internal demonstration;
- next three workflow candidates;
- 30/60/90-day roadmap.

# 12. Capstone acceptance criteria

A participant does not complete the program merely because Codex generated something impressive.

The capstone passes only when:

1. A real business trigger starts it.
2. It consumes real or representative business input.
3. It produces an output somebody actually uses.
4. Ten test cases have been run.
5. Missing or unsafe inputs cause a controlled failure.
6. Human approval exists at consequential decision points.
7. Output and evidence are saved predictably.
8. Another team member can operate it from the runbook.
9. The previous working version can be restored.
10. At least one baseline metric is measured.

This is the participant-level equivalent of DataOps’s principle that “done requires proof.”

# 13. Good participant projects

The first workflow should:

- happen at least weekly;
- consume meaningful owner or senior-operator time;
- have examples of previous outputs;
- have a clear beginning and end;
- produce something reviewable;
- tolerate a human approval step;
- be reversible;
- avoid money movement, legal decisions, sensitive HR decisions, or unsupervised external communication.

Bad first project:

> “Run my entire marketing department.”

Good first project:

> “Prepare our weekly client performance pack, identify anomalies, draft the commentary, and queue it for account-manager approval.”

## 13.1 Education/community examples

### Guest/speaker operations

Intake → research → briefing document → missing-information checklist → follow-up draft → event asset pack → human approval.

### Weekly community operations brief

Collect unanswered questions, upcoming events, member issues, overdue actions, and engagement signals → produce a prioritized operator brief.

### Course-feedback intelligence

Collect survey responses, support messages, and session notes → categorize issues → identify repeated confusion → propose curriculum changes → link recommendations to evidence.

### Member-support triage

Classify questions → retrieve relevant material → draft a response → escalate sensitive/novel cases → log unresolved themes.

### Content preparation pipeline

Recorded session → identify useful ideas → draft channel-specific assets → editorial approval → preserve links to source.

## 13.2 Agency/consulting examples

### Weekly client reporting

Collect metrics → validate completeness → identify anomalies → prepare narrative → flag decisions → queue for account-manager approval.

### Client onboarding

Process intake → detect missing information → create project structure → prepare kickoff brief → draft missing-information follow-up.

### Meeting-to-execution workflow

Convert notes/recordings into decisions, owners, deadlines, dependencies, and follow-up drafts; require human confirmation before updating external systems.

### Proposal preparation

Turn discovery notes and existing case material into scoped proposal, assumptions, risks, and unanswered questions.

# 14. Teaching nonprogrammers

## 14.1 Default to the Codex desktop app, not the terminal

The Codex desktop app is better for the target audience because it presents Codex as a workspace for supervising agent work: project threads, visual review of changes, comments on diffs, worktree isolation, reusable skills, and an automation review queue.

Source:
https://openai.com/index/introducing-the-codex-app/

The terminal should be optional/advanced.

## 14.2 Provide a prepared workspace

Example structure:

```text
00_START_HERE.md
01_BUSINESS_CONTEXT.md
02_WORKFLOW_SPEC.md
03_DEFINITION_OF_DONE.md
AGENTS.md

examples/
  good/
  bad/

tests/
inputs/
outputs/
runbook/
```

Teach participants these mental models:

- file = durable brief;
- folder = work area;
- version history = undo button + audit trail;
- test = example with expected result;
- skill = reusable SOP plus resources/tools;
- automation = recurring job with review queue;
- connector/API = controlled doorway to another system.

## 14.3 Teach one repeated operating protocol

Every Codex task follows:

1. **Inspect:** Read the workspace and explain current state. Do not change anything.
2. **Plan:** Propose the smallest safe change.
3. **Build:** Make only the approved change.
4. **Test:** Run supplied examples and edge cases.
5. **Explain:** Describe what changed in plain English.
6. **Review:** Show risks, assumptions, and human decisions.
7. **Record:** Update runbook/decision log.

A consistent protocol is more valuable than a giant prompt library.

## 14.4 Separate ChatGPT and Codex roles

### ChatGPT Pro = thinking room

Use for:

- process interviews;
- research;
- workflow mapping;
- prioritization;
- analysis of examples;
- writing specifications;
- generating tests;
- evaluating results;
- retrospectives;
- explaining unfamiliar concepts.

### Codex = work room

Use for:

- persistent files;
- repeatable processes;
- scripts/lightweight internal tools;
- document/spreadsheet operations;
- running tests;
- reusable skills;
- scheduling stable workflows;
- maintaining project state over time.

## 14.5 Minimum technical vocabulary

Participants need only a small vocabulary:

- files/folders;
- inputs/outputs;
- version/rollback;
- test/expected result;
- read vs write permission;
- local vs cloud;
- credential;
- connector;
- log;
- schedule;
- failure/retry.

Do not begin with APIs, JSON, MCP, RAG, vector databases, or multi-agent orchestration. Introduce technical concepts only when a visible project need requires them.

# 16. Privacy and safety

Privacy must be taught early rather than as an appendix.

For personal Plus/Pro workspaces, model-training data sharing is enabled by default but can be turned off in Data Controls. Codex has additional full-environment training settings. ChatGPT Business/Enterprise data is not used to train OpenAI models by default.

Source:
https://openai.com/business-data/

Recommended rules:

- use synthetic/sanitized data in early exercises;
- turn off model-training data sharing in personal workspaces;
- review Codex-specific controls;
- use Business workspace for sensitive company workflows;
- never put credentials in prompts or ordinary project files;
- start with read-only access;
- require human approval before external messages, record changes, publishing, money movement, or other consequential actions.

# 17. Instructor proof strategy

The instructor’s own story should become the strongest case study.

Do not frame the credibility as:

> “DataOps is technically sophisticated.”

Frame it as:

> **We run an operation that appears to require a much larger team, while preserving human control where it matters. Here is the measured evidence and the method we use.**

Instrument the operation before launch.

| Credibility metric | What to record |
|---|---|
| Operational scope | Communities, courses, platforms, events, newsletters, podcasts, sponsors |
| Human capacity | Number of people and approximate human hours |
| Agent capacity | Runs, tasks, workflows, artifacts produced |
| Cycle time | Before vs after representative workflows |
| Follow-up performance | Overdue items, unanswered requests, time to response |
| Quality | Revision rate, rejected outputs, missing evidence |
| Reliability | Successful runs, failed runs, retries, human interventions |
| Cost | Model/tool spend per workflow |
| Leverage | Output/operations per human team member |

The external public stories support the thesis, but the instructor’s operation should provide the deepest proof.

# 19. Discovery and validation plan

Before recording a full curriculum, run a two-week discovery sprint.

## Interview targets

Interview 12 prospects across:

- education/community/media businesses;
- agencies;
- consultancies/professional services.

Aim for a mix of founders and operations leaders.

## Interview questions

Avoid “Would you buy an AI automation course?”

Ask:

- Tell me about a repetitive process in your business that annoys you.
- Walk me through the last time it happened.
- Who does each step?
- Where does information come from?
- What do you copy/paste?
- Who do you wait on?
- How often does it happen?
- How many hours per month does it consume?
- What goes wrong?
- What have you tried to automate?
- Why did that succeed or fail?
- Would you build it internally, hire someone, or buy software?
- Who owns the budget?
- What would fixing it be worth?

## Strong validation

The strongest validation is paid implementation, not survey interest.

A good next experiment is to run the Week 1 workflow audit with five paid founding companies and observe:

- whether they can identify sufficiently valuable workflows;
- whether nontechnical people can create good Agent Briefs;
- where they get stuck with Codex;
- what technical concepts actually need teaching;
- how much instructor involvement is required;
- whether one live workflow produces enough economic value to justify $10k.

> Most founders already use ChatGPT. Very few have actually integrated AI into how their company runs.
>
> The problem is not prompting. The problem is that the business itself is usually scattered across inboxes, spreadsheets, SOPs, calendars, SaaS tools, and the founder’s memory.
>
> I run two communities and many courses across many platforms with two people and a team of agents. I built an internal operating system to make recurring work explicit, assign work to humans and AI, require proof, surface follow-ups, and keep operations moving.
>
> You do not need to copy my software. In four weeks, I will help you take one recurring workflow in your own company and build the simpler equivalent: mapped, specified, tested, automated where appropriate, human-supervised, and measurable.
>
> You leave with one real workflow running and a method you can reuse on the rest of the business.

# 21. Current recommendation

Build the flagship around this proposition:

> ## **Build one AI-run workflow in four weeks**
> Turn a recurring process that depends on you into a tested workflow your team and AI can operate together—without becoming a programmer.

Use DataOps as the principal case study and reference framework. Use external transformation stories to show the movement is larger than one organization. Teach ChatGPT and Codex as components of an operating method rather than as the product itself.

The durable category is:

> **AI-native operations for founder-led businesses.**

The durable capability participants should leave with is:

> **The ability to repeatedly transform a messy recurring process into a specified, tested, human-supervised AI workflow.**

That is substantially more defensible—and more compatible with a $10,000 company-level price—than selling access to a tool curriculum.
