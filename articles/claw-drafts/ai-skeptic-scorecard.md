# The Skeptic Scorecard: Dan Luu Audited Ed Zitron, and It Wasn't Close

> Subtitle: The most-cited AI skeptic's predictions, checked against reality: peak AI six times, financial forecasts called crimes that came true, and a track record that should change how you pick your sources. With a scorecard you can run on anyone, boosters included.

[IMAGE: A scorecard table with dated predictions on the left and outcomes on the right, nearly every outcome column marked wrong, one row marked right for contrast]
Caption: 1. Dated, falsifiable predictions. 2. Outcomes from primary sources. 3. The score.

The second most-discussed story on Hacker News this week, 857 points and [1,032 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49526069), is an essay by [Dan Luu](https://danluu.com/zitron/) asking a simple question: how accurate have Ed Zitron's predictions been? Zitron, the author of *Where's Your Ed At* and host of *Better Offline*, is the most widely cited AI skeptic working today, the writer people link when they want a numbers person who says the whole thing is a scam.

Luu's method is disarming. He discloses his own position first: no financial stake in AI companies, a failed phone screen at an AI lab years ago, a 2022 [audit of futurist predictions](https://danluu.com/futurist-predictions/) that found the optimists wrong on results and reasoning, and a 2015 essay arguing people *underestimate* AI's ability to displace jobs. His stated position is the boring one: if something is currently happening, people saying it can never happen are probably wrong.

Then he checks the record. Dated claims, primary sources, revenue filings. The result is one of the most one-sided scorecards you will read this year, and the interesting part is what it teaches about how all of us consume AI commentary.

### The scorecard

A sample, with Zitron's calls on the left and reality on the right:

- "We're reaching the upper limits of what generative AI can do" ([Feb 2024](https://www.wheresyoured.at/sam-altman-fried/)). Models have improved substantially every year since.
- "Have we reached peak AI?" ([March 2024](https://www.wheresyoured.at/peakai/)), a prediction he re-upped at least six times through 2024 and 2025. Still no peak.
- OpenAI's forecast of $11.6B revenue for 2025 was "a statement so egregious that I am surprised it's not some kind of financial crime to say it out loud" ([Oct 2024](https://www.wheresyoured.at/oai-business/)). OpenAI exceeded the forecast.
- Gemini reaching 500M users by end of 2025 was "a number so unrealistic that someone at Google should have been fired, and that someone is Sundar Pichai" ([Feb 2025](https://www.wheresyoured.at/wheres-the-money/)). Gemini passed 750M.
- Anthropic making $34.5B in 2027 was "laughable on many levels" ([Feb 2025](https://www.wheresyoured.at/longcon/)). Anthropic's 2026 ARR already runs past the number that was laughable.
- CoreWeave was "rotten" and unlikely to survive six months without fundraising ([March 2025](https://www.wheresyoured.at/core-incompetency/)). The stock trades at more than double its IPO price.
- Cursor was "going to die," and a $10B price was implausible ([Aug 2025](https://www.wheresyoured.at/ai-is-a-money-trap/)). Cursor sold for $60B.
- The AI bubble would pop "no later than Q2 2026." It is September 2026.
- And the detailed case study: a November 2024 talk arguing Meta, Google, and Microsoft are "dying" companies "shoving AI into everything" because they've run out of ways to grow. Luu answers with the filings: Meta's revenue went $135B to $201B between 2023 and 2025 with first-half 2026 up 30%, and Alphabet and Microsoft show the same shape. The "dying" thesis rests on a cherry-picked third-party traffic estimate and a theory that Google search was ruined by one villain.

Read enough of the list and a pattern emerges beyond the misses. The reasoning fails even where the outcome might eventually cooperate, and the confidence never wavers. Luu notes he has made plenty of wrong predictions himself, with confidence levels attached, and has never once been wrong at the certainty Zitron attaches to every call. Routine wrongness at maximum stated confidence is a calibration signature, and it's measurable.

### The mechanics of the genre

The essay's most useful contribution is the anatomy of how this works, because the pattern predates Zitron and will outlive him.

**Authority laundering.** People cite Zitron as "the guy who looked at the numbers." When Luu follows the numbers down, the spreadsheet behind Zitron's Anthropic projection contains [February 30th, March counted twice, and a two-month stretch counted as one](https://x.com/binarybits/status/2034377838883700953) (found by Timothy B. Lee); fix the arithmetic and the "smoking gun" resolves. A [longtime HN commenter](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45402639) who tried debunking individual claims describes articles built as ten-thousand-word floods where "basically everything is made up or misrepresented," with the links not saying what the text implies.

**The gish gallop as a denial-of-service attack.** Luu's phrase: refuting ninety seconds of the talk takes five thousand words, and generating it took Zitron nothing. Nonsense is cheap to produce and expensive to correct, so correction loses on volume. If you have ever closed a tab because fact-checking an argument would take your whole afternoon, you have felt the mechanism working.

**Anger as the product.** The audience gets someone who shares the anger and appears to bring receipts. Michał Zalewski's explanation, quoted by Luu: crisp, strong positions build followings, and the feedback loop gradually self-radicalizes until the position is an identity. Which explains the last piece of the machine.

**The defender moves.** Luu read the discussions and catalogued four recurring defenses: "you didn't address X" (the gallop again), flat denial that the refuted prediction was ever made (the dated links say otherwise), "what about all the wrong boosters?" (Luu's futurist audit already covered them; both sides being wrong makes each side exactly as wrong as before), and "he's just early," which in this case requires believing 2026 models are no better than 2023's. The subreddit's own moderation reportedly bans benchmark posts, which keeps the chamber clean.

### The mirror

Here Luu is careful, and it's why the essay lands. Zitron is the flip side of Ray Kurzweil, whose famous "86% prediction accuracy" [turns out to be 7%](https://danluu.com/futurist-predictions/) when you check the cited predictions. Same trick, opposite direction: numbers as an aura of credibility, dates that slide, fans who never audit. The closer historical parallel is Paul Ehrlich, who predicted imminent civilizational famine in 1968, was wrong for fifty straight years, and said in 2015 that his language "would be even more apocalyptic today." Being loudly wrong, at scale, with an audience that defends you, is a career model with a long pedigree.

And to answer the obvious complaint: good skeptical voices exist. Luu points to [whitequark](https://social.treehouse.systems/@whitequark), an anti-AI writer whose reasoning is sound enough that disagreement feels like different premises rather than different realities. The tell is the audience size. Nuance gets no podcast invites.

**Tone decides who gets read. The track record should decide who gets believed. Those are different people, and confusing them is expensive.**

### The part defenders get right

Fairness requires saying this plainly: the audit kills the predictions, and it leaves some arguments standing. The HN thread's best counterpoint is that Zitron's economic themes, heavy capex against thin revenue, circular financing between the labs and their investors, remain real and debated, and Luu's essay does not resolve them. A crash could still come; companies could still fail; Luu says so himself, noting that even a total AI crash would leave society changed by the capabilities already shipped, whatever happens to any particular company's valuation.

Also worth noting: Luu is nobody's booster. In the same period he has publicly dismissed METR's headline progress benchmark as unmeaningful, which is the kind of call that annoys the pro-AI side just as much. The essay is an audit, and audits are symmetric or worthless.

### The scorecard you can run on anyone

The artifact. Five questions, applicable to any AI commentator, skeptic or booster, and honestly to this blog too:

- [ ] **Are the predictions dated and falsifiable?** "This is a bubble" is content. "The bubble pops no later than Q2 2026" is a prediction you can score.
- [ ] **Is a confidence level attached, and does it ever drop?** Maximum confidence on every call means the confidence carries no information.
- [ ] **Do the numbers support the claim they're attached to?** Pick one citation per piece and follow it to the primary source. One session of this teaches you more about a writer than ten posts of theirs.
- [ ] **What would change their mind?** A writer with no stated disconfirming condition is describing an identity, and identities don't update.
- [ ] **Run the same scorecard on the other side.** Booster timelines, adoption forecasts, "X is dead" proclamations. Both camps fail this test regularly, and the double standard is how echo chambers recruit.

And the practice that beats all five: keep your own prediction log. Dated claims, confidence, outcomes. Engineers I respect do this for their systems; commentators deserve the same evals. Calibration is just evals for humans.

### Close

Here's what I believe: the useful lesson of the Zitron audit is aimed at readers, and at me, more than at one angry writer. All of us are pattern-matching on tone, tribe, and confidence, because checking primary sources is slow and boring. The teams and careers that navigate the next few years well will be the ones that scorecard their sources, both the doom chorus and the rocket parade, and log their own calls with dates attached. I have made wrong calls in this newsletter, and some of you have corrected me in the comments, which is the scorecard working. Keep doing that. Trust the track record, demand the date, and be suspicious of anyone whose confidence never dips, especially in September 2026, when the bubble was due to pop no later than Q2.
